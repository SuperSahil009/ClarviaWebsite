Understanding substance use

When something that once helped starts feeling harder to control.

Alcohol or other drugs can become connected to stress, relief, habits, social situations or difficult emotions. Not everyone who uses a substance has a substance use disorder; support can be useful at many stages.

Dependence may involve physical or psychological adaptation, while problematic use can affect health, relationships or daily life. Assessment and diagnosis, where needed, should be provided by appropriately qualified professionals.

01

Recognising patterns

Notice how and when substance use has become part of everyday coping, relationships or routines.

02

Understanding triggers

Explore stress, emotions, people or circumstances associated with cravings or use.

03

Considering change

Explore your own reasons for making changes, even if you feel unsure or conflicted.

04

Building practical tools

Develop safer coping strategies, a support network and a plan for challenging situations.

Understanding readiness

Change rarely happens in a straight line.

The stages-of-change model is one way of understanding how motivation and behaviour can shift over time. These stages are not rigid steps or a test of someone's readiness.

01

Not yet considering

Change may not feel necessary or possible.

02

Thinking it over

You may notice concerns and feel mixed emotions.

03

Getting ready

You might explore options, supports or a small first step.

04

Taking action

You experiment with new behaviours and support.

05

Maintaining progress

You practise skills and make adjustments over time.

Setbacks aren't a verdict on you.

People may move back and forth between stages. Returning to substance use can be an opportunity to review supports and strategies, without shame. Additional medical or specialist help may be needed.

Family & loved ones

You can care about someone without carrying everything alone.

Worrying about a person's substance use may bring confusion, exhaustion and uncertainty. Family guidance can focus on making sense of what's happening, supporting communication, developing boundaries and looking after your own wellbeing.

We can explore how to approach difficult conversations and which external services or specialist resources might be appropriate. You do not need to wait for your loved one to start treatment to seek guidance for yourself.

Group or family support sessions may be considered by arrangement, suitability and demand. No recurring group programme is currently advertised.
